      What the app sees

      Real frames from our own filming. Tap one and the pose model runs on it right here, on this device, and draws what it found. The orange line is the torso line the score is built on.

      How to film a lap the app can read

      The pose model scores only frames where it can see both shoulders and both hips. Six habits, straight out of our test footage.

      1. Stand on the side, not the end. The app measures the angle between shoulders and hips, so it needs a side view.
      2. Crouch on the deck, a little above the water. Not standing height, which tilts the body in the picture, and not right at the surface, which hides the hips: in our study freestyle filmed from the surface read in 12 to 26% of frames, the same stroke filmed close and slightly above the water read in 90%.
      3. 3 to 5 metres away, landscape. Close enough that the swimmer is a decent size in the frame, far enough to fit a few strokes.
      4. Start after the push-off, stop before the wall. Standing frames are ignored anyway, but the clip stays short and quick to score.
      5. One swimmer in the shot. The app follows the most confident person it finds. A crowded lane confuses it.
      6. Film from the same spot each time. The body-line number depends on where the phone was. Backstroke and back float read best; for freestyle, compare laps filmed the same way and watch the trend rather than one number.

      Which situations the phone model reads

      From running our six sample clips through the app's own code (test/run_clips_yolo.js), October 2026.

      • Treading water99%
      • Body dropped, head back71%
      • Backstroke lap, phone on deck33% · scored 90
      • Standing at the wall30% · upright, not scored
      • Freestyle from the deck7–18% · refused

      The app needs the body in at least 30% of frames and at least one second of real swimming, travelling and not upright, before it will give a number. Across our 87-clip study, about four in ten swimming clips filmed from the deck pass that bar; the rest get an honest refusal instead of a guess.

          Why trust the number

          Before writing any screens we ran a pose model over 31 clips we filmed ourselves (16 swimming, 5 treading, 10 acted struggling) and asked one question: does the body angle actually separate flat swimming from a dropped body?

          Tap a dot. Each one is a clip: height is the median body angle, darker means the model could read more of its frames.

          It does. When the swimmer is actually swimming, the body reads between 10° and 43°; a vertical body reads 60° to 88°. The swimming dots up near 80° are clips where the kid spent most of the time standing at the wall, which is why the app throws away frames where the shoulders are not travelling. What the model struggles with is seeing the torso at all when the swimmer is face down in splash, so the app refuses to score instead of guessing.

          What the research says

          Swimmers' trunks sit 10° to 15° from flat at everyday speeds, and front crawl and backstroke are the same within a degree or two (Zamparo and colleagues 2009; Gonjo and colleagues 2023). Novice 8 to 13-year-olds measured 2° to 14°, and the angle fell as they improved (Jerszyński and colleagues 2013). A phone held on the deck looks down at the water, which adds to the angle in the picture: our own clearly flat laps read 11° to 32°. So the app's bands are set about 10° above the papers, and every reading is quoted to ±5°, the error found when 2D pose keypoints are checked against lab motion capture on land. Children stroke faster than adults, about 35 cycles a minute at an easy pace (Kjendlie and colleagues 2004), which is why the rhythm band reaches 1.5 strokes a second per arm. The full list of sources is in the repository, feasibility/v04/LITERATURE.md.

          Honest limits

          • The body-line bands lean on published adult and novice studies, shifted for a deck-height phone; the head-position and rhythm bands are our own and are not validated. The absolute number moves with camera height, so compare laps filmed from the same spot; a strong freestyler filmed from slightly above can read 30° and be told the hips look low.
          • Frames where the body is steeper than 60° (standing, treading, at the wall) are counted but never scored. If that is most of what the app saw, it says so instead of scoring. A lap that reads steeper than 45° the whole way is refused too: that is a phone held high, at the end of the lane or behind the swimmer, not sinking hips. Film from the side, near the water.
          • Freestyle filmed from standing height is mostly unreadable with the phone model.
          • The app follows one person. It is not a lifeguard and never will be. The live coach and the float test are the same model, so they share the same limits.

          Privacy and settings

          Where your video goes: nowhere. The clip is opened by the browser on this device, the pose model runs here, and only the scores (a few numbers and a date) are kept in this browser's storage. Close the tab and the video is gone. The live camera is never recorded. A shared link carries numbers only.

          Consent

          Film only swimmers you are responsible for, or who have agreed. For anyone under 18 that means a parent or guardian is doing the filming or has said yes. Do not film other people's children in the background if you can avoid it, and never share a clip without asking.

          Two things to know

          Video never leaves this device. The app reads the swimmer's body position right here and keeps only the scores.

          Film your own swimmer. For a child, that means you are the parent or guardian, or have their say-so.

          SafeDive Coach is a swim-form helper. It is not a safety device and does not watch for drowning. An adult must always be watching the water.
